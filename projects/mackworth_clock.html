The Mackworth Clock

During WWII, RAF radar operators kept missing U-boat blips near the end of long watches. Norman Mackworth built a clock to study the problem in the lab. Its pointer ticks forward once a second, for a very long time. Every so often it jumps twice as far. Your job is to notice.

He found that people's ability to spot those double jumps sagged within the first half hour, even though they were trying hard. That sag, called the vigilance decrement, is still one of the sturdiest findings in human factors.

This version gives you some help: ARGUS-1, an automated detector that lights up when it thinks it saw a double jump. How good is ARGUS? That's hidden, and it differs from run to run. Between rounds, you'll rate how much you trust it. At the end, you'll see whether your trust followed how good it really was.

  1. Watch the pointer. Each tick moves it one mark. A double jump moves it two.
  2. When you see a double jump, press Space, click the dial, or tap the button. You have about three seconds.
  3. ARGUS will flag what it thinks are double jumps. Use it however you like. Only your presses count.

Why watching is hard

Paying attention to something where nothing happens is tiring work, not rest. Over a long watch, people get worse at spotting signals and slower to react to them. The modern view is that sustained attention uses up mental resources. Boredom also makes the mind wander. Both cut into how much attention is left for the task.

Why automation doesn't simply fix it

Hand the watching to a machine and new problems show up. People go along with its false alarms (automation bias), stop checking what it misses (complacency), or write it off after a few mistakes (disuse). The goal isn't maximum trust. It's calibrated trust: relying on the system about as much as it deserves.
